Programming TO Exceptions
by Shiv Kumar
Part 1 - Core Philosophy
Takes long pause, scanning the room
You know what's funny about error handling? We all think we're doing it right. I certainly did. For years, I was that developer - sprinkling if-checks like confetti, logging errors with a confidence that bordered on arrogance. 'Error occurred!' Mimics typing There, handled! Wry smile
Want to know what shattered that confidence? Production. Pauses
Production doesn't care about your clever error handling. Production is that brutal teacher who shows up at 3 AM with pop quizzes you never studied for. Silent failures. Data inconsistencies. Systems running happily along while everything is quietly, thoroughly going wrong.
Walks to other side of stage
I remember the exact moment it hit me. Four hours into debugging a critical issue, staring at logs filled with 'Error occurred!' messages. Helpful, right? Audience chuckles That's when I realized - my entire approach to error handling wasn't just wrong, it was... backwards.
See, we were treating errors like unwanted guests. Something to catch, log, and usher out the door as quickly as possible. 'Nothing to see here, folks!' But production... production taught me that errors are more like messengers. And we were shooting the messenger. Every. Single. Time.
Leans forward
But here's where it gets interesting. Over the next decade, across dozens of production systems, through countless sleepless nights and emergency fixes, a pattern started emerging. The systems that truly stood the test of time? They weren't the ones with the most sophisticated error handling. They were the ones that embraced errors. Not as problems to handle, but as tools to harness.
Pauses, takes a sip of water
I'm about to share something that might sound crazy at first. A completely different way of thinking about exceptions. Not as errors to catch, but as allies to embrace. A philosophy I call 'Programming TO Exceptions.'
And before you dismiss this as just another 'best practice' talk - every pattern, every practice I'm about to share has been battle-tested in production. Not just by me, but by teams across multiple organizations, multiple domains, multiple scales. This isn't theory. This is survival knowledge.
Steps closer to audience
Ready to rethink everything you know about error handling?
Part 2 - Fundamental Principles
Moves to center stage, becomes more contemplative
You know what's fascinating about mistakes? Not the mistakes themselves, but the moment you realize you've been making them. Pauses For years.
I was so proud of our error handling. Try-catch blocks everywhere. Logging in every corner. A safety net so fine, nothing could slip through. Dry laugh At least, that's what I thought.
Steps forward with purpose
But before I tell you about making exceptions smarter, there's something fundamental we need to talk about. Something that changed everything about how I viewed exceptions.
Pauses, looks around room intently
Exceptions. Do. Not. Return.
Lets that sink in
This isn't just syntax. This is philosophy. When you throw an exception, you're not returning a value. You're not suggesting something might be wrong. You're making a statement: 'This operation cannot continue.' Full stop.
Moves to side of stage
See, for years I treated exceptions like fancy return codes. Catch here, check there, maybe return null, maybe return false... Shakes head I was missing the point entirely.
When your code throws an exception, it's not asking for permission. It's not saying 'hey, something might be wrong, what do you think?' It's declaring, with absolute certainty: 'I cannot fulfill this contract.'
Steps forward with renewed energy
And this brings us to something equally fundamental - something that will transform how you think about method design entirely.
Pauses, draws imaginary line in the air
In a system built on Programming TO Exceptions, every method falls into one of two categories. Just two. Action methods and Query methods.
Moves to one side of stage
Action methods DO things. They make changes. They update state. And here's the key - they don't return anything. Their contract is simple: if they return, they succeeded. If they don't... well, you'll hear about it through an exception.
Becomes animated
Think about this common flow: gestures as if writing code
Copy
EnsureOrderRequestIsValid(orderRequest) # Action - throws if invalid
order = TransformToOrder(orderRequest) # Query - transforms to domain model
SaveOrder(order) # Action - persists to DB
EnsureCustomerCanBeNotified(order.CustomerId) # Action - throws if can't notify
notification = BuildOrderNotification(order) # Query - builds notification content
SendCustomerNotification(notification) # Action - sends notification
PublishOrderCreatedEvent(order) # Action - publishes event
Points to imaginary code
See how the intent is crystal clear? Notice that 'Ensure' prefix on our validation methods? That's not just a naming convention - it's a contract. It tells you immediately: this method will throw if its condition isn't met. No return values, no status codes, just a pure guarantee.
Moves to other side of stage
Then we have Query methods. Their contract is equally clear - they return exactly what they promise. Not null. Not empty strings. Not special error codes. What they promise in their signature is what you get.
Leans forward
"But what if the query can't fulfill its contract?" Ah! Then it doesn't return at all. It throws an exception. Because returning null when you promised an Order isn't fulfilling the contract. It's breaking it.
Steps to center, speaking deliberately
Now, here's where it gets beautiful. When you embrace these principles, something magical happens to your code. Look at any method, and you'll see... just the happy path.
Pauses for effect
Because if execution reached this line of code, everything before it succeeded. No ifs. No buts. No checking return codes. No defensive programming. Just clean, clear, purposeful code.
This isn't just about clean code. This is about confidence. About certainty. About knowing, without a doubt, that if your code is executing, it's executing correctly.
Straightens up
Now... small smile now we can talk about making exceptions smarter.
Part 3 - Making Exceptions Smarter
Moves center stage
But making exceptions smarter... that journey took time. And like most good stories, it started with pain. Wry smile
You see, exceptions gave us stack traces. Great! Points you to where it broke. But why? What was the state? What data caused it? Shakes head Stack traces alone were like trying to solve a murder mystery with just the crime scene location. No weapon, no motive, no suspects.
Paces thoughtfully
At first, we just added better messages. Then status codes. Counts on fingers Then severity levels. But something was still missing. We were still debugging in the dark.
Stops suddenly
Then came the revelation. What if exceptions weren't just error signals? What if they were... pauses debug packages? Complete snapshots of what went wrong?
Becomes animated
We started small. Added the basic properties - status codes, severity levels, reason phrases. Then came what I call the 'action intent' - does this need user intervention? System intervention? Manual support? Gestures expansively
But the game-changer? Context. Leans in Rich, deep, meaningful context. Method parameters, local variables, system state - anything that could help reproduce the issue. When you're debugging at 3 AM, that context is worth its weight in gold.
Steps back, serious tone
Now, I know what some of you are thinking. 'That's a lot of overhead.' Pauses And you're right. It is. But let me ask you something...
Moves to edge of stage
What costs more? Taking the time to craft rich, meaningful exceptions? Or having your entire team spend hours - or days - trying to reproduce and debug issues with nothing but a stack trace and a prayer?
Straightens jacket
But here's where it gets really interesting. Once we had these rich exceptions, we discovered something unexpected. Something that changed how we thought about system observability entirely.
Dramatic pause
We didn't need INFO logs anymore.
Watches audience reaction
I know, I know. Sounds crazy, right? 'No INFO logs? How do you know what's happening in your system?' Smiles
Let me tell you a secret: In fifteen years of running production systems this way, I've never missed them. Not once. When your exceptions are rich with context, when your validation is rock solid at your boundaries, when your error messages tell the complete story... INFO logs become noise. Pure noise.
Moves center stage
But to make this work, your exceptions need to be first-class citizens in your system. And I mean first-class.
Leans forward, speaking intently
You know what keeps me awake at night now? Not production issues. No. What keeps me awake is reviewing exception messages. Two-hour discussions about a single error message? Absolutely. Excessive? Pauses Not even close.
Because here's what I learned the hard way: your exception message isn't just technical output - it's your system's moment of truth. It's your chance to clearly explain what went wrong, why it went wrong, and often most importantly, how to fix it.
Takes a few steps, gathering thoughts
Let me give you an example. Which message would you rather see?
'PaymentPlanValidation failed'
Waits a beat
Or:
'Payment plan amount $5000 exceeds maximum allowed limit of $3000 for Basic tier accounts. Available tiers: Premium ($10000 limit), Enterprise (unlimited)'
Looks at audience
See the difference? One leaves everyone guessing. The other empowers immediate action. One generates support tickets. The other... smiles the other lets users solve their own problems.
Part 4 - Structure and Strategy
Takes deliberate steps forward
But clarity isn't just about messages. It's about structure. Organization. Hierarchy.
Projects exception hierarchy diagram
Take a look at this. Pauses This isn't just a diagram. This is a battle plan.
Points to top level
At the root, we have our base exception. Every exception in our system inherits from this. Slight smile And yes, I mean every exception. No rogue exceptions allowed in our house.
Moves hand down to next level
Then we split. Business exceptions on one side, technical exceptions on the other. Pauses Clean separation of concerns. Because a validation failure is fundamentally different from a service timeout, and your system should know the difference.
Looks intently at audience
But here's where it gets interesting. See these concrete exceptions at the bottom? Notice something? They're hyper-specific. One exception, one scenario, one place in the code. No generic error classes, no reused exceptions. When you see a 'PayPlanValidationException', you know exactly where to look.
Steps back
"Isn't that excessive?" I hear that a lot. Shakes head No. It's precise. It's intentional. It's clarity through specificity.
Moves to center stage
And this precision pays off at the boundaries. Remember those boundaries we talked about? This is where the magic happens.
Gestures to indicate system boundaries
At our boundaries - our API endpoints, our service interfaces - we have exactly two catches. Two. Our base exception, and... pauses everything else.
Leans forward
Think about that for a minute. Every exception we throw is caught by that first catch. We know its properties, its context, its meaning. We can translate it perfectly into the right response, the right message, the right action.
And that second catch? Grim smile That's our safety net. Anything we didn't anticipate. Anything we didn't throw ourselves. Those are potential bugs, and they get treated accordingly - logged as critical, returned as 500 errors, escalated for immediate attention.
Paces thoughtfully
But here's the beautiful part - at our boundaries, we're not just catching exceptions. We're enriching them. Because at the boundary, we have something precious - the complete input state that triggered this whole operation.
Moves to whiteboard
Think about it. Your exception arrives with all its accumulated context, and now you add the initial request that started it all. Every parameter, every header, every bit of context - all becoming searchable properties in your logs.
Draws quick diagram
Status codes, severity levels, action intents, business context - all first-class citizens in your observability system. Want to alert on all failed payment validations for premium customers? One query. Need to track all timeout errors from a specific downstream service? It's right there.
Steps back to center
But here's the beautiful part. Over time, that second catch... it gets lonely. Because when you're Programming TO Exceptions, when you're thinking in terms of explicit failure modes, when you're making every exception count... those unexpected errors become rare. Really rare.
Stops, turns to audience
And that's when you know you've turned a corner. When your error handling isn't about catching problems anymore. It's about expressing intent. About making failure modes explicit. About turning exceptions from surprises into... guarantees.
Steps forward
But to make this work, you need one more crucial piece. You need to understand how to handle exceptions meaningfully...
Part 5 - Meaningful Handling
Moves to center stage, contemplative
Let's talk about what it means to handle an exception. Pauses Actually, let me rephrase that. Let's talk about what it doesn't mean.
Starts counting on fingers
Catching and logging? That's not handling. That's notification. Catching and rethrowing? That's not handling. That's passing the buck. Empty catch blocks? Shudders dramatically That's not handling. That's burying problems alive.
Faces audience directly
Early in my career, I thought I was handling exceptions. Try-catch, log a message - 'Error occurred!' - and call it a day. Shakes head slowly I was so wrong.
Moves to side of stage
You see, true handling... that's about taking meaningful action. Let me say that again: Meaningful. Action.
Begins pacing thoughtfully
What's meaningful action? Retrying a failed network call with exponential backoff - that's handling. Switching to a backup service when the primary fails - that's handling. Implementing a circuit breaker to prevent cascade failures - absolutely handling.
Stops suddenly
But the most meaningful handling? It happens at your boundaries. And this is where it gets really interesting.
Moves to whiteboard
When an exception hits your boundary, you have something incredibly powerful - a complete reproduction package. Draws diagram Think about what you have:
The original exception, rich with context from every layer it passed through. The complete input state that triggered the operation. The system state at the boundary itself.
Turns to audience, excited
Do you realize what this means? Pauses Every exception becomes a complete reproduction recipe.
Steps forward
"Cannot reproduce" becomes a phrase of the past. Because now, when a problem hits production, you don't just have an error message. You have everything you need to replay that exact scenario in your development environment.
Moves to computer setup

Returns to center
And this changes everything about how we handle production issues. No more "works on my machine". No more "can't reproduce". No more wild guessing about what the user was doing when it failed.
Moves to side of stage
This is why we put so much effort into enriching our exceptions at the boundary. We're not just logging errors - we're creating perfect snapshots of failure scenarios. Complete, reproducible, actionable snapshots.
Speaks more intensely
And when you combine this with structured logging and proper observability... pauses That's when things get really powerful.
Draws new diagram
Every exception property becomes a dimension you can query. Every piece of context becomes a filter you can apply. Want to find all payment validation failures for premium customers in the last hour? Snaps fingers Done. Want to alert on any order processing that fails after payment success? Simple query.
Steps back
But remember - this only works because we've made our exceptions rich, structured, and meaningful. Because we've turned them from simple error messages into complete debug packages.
Moves center stage
Which brings me to one of my most controversial rules: If you can't take meaningful action, don't catch the exception. Pauses At all.
Watches audience reaction
I know what you're thinking. "But what about logging? What about monitoring?" Smiles Those aren't actions. Those are observations. Important observations, yes, but not handling.
Leans forward
Here's the thing about letting exceptions propagate: they carry their context with them. Every layer they pass through is another chance to add information, to enrich the story. When they finally reach a boundary where real handling can occur - now you have the complete picture.
Part 6 - Fail Fast and Fail Visibly
Takes deliberate steps to center stage
Let me tell you about one of the most powerful principles I've learned: Fail Fast and Fail Visibly.
Gestures to create an imaginary house
Think of your system like a house. You've got a front door where users come in. A back door where you talk to other services. Maybe some windows where configuration sneaks in. Pauses Where do you check for intruders?
Waits for audience reaction
At the doors! Emphatic gesture You don't let suspicious characters wander around your house hoping they'll behave. You stop them right at the entrance.
Moves to side of stage
This is what I mean by Failing Fast. Validate everything at your system boundaries. User input? Check it before it gets anywhere near your business logic. Configuration? Validate it the moment you load it. Response from a downstream service? Verify it before you process it.
Leans forward
"But wait," you might say, "isn't that defensive programming? Didn't you just tell us to avoid that?" Knowing smile
Paces thoughtfully
No, this is different. This isn't about peppering your business logic with guards and checks. This is about having strong, clear boundaries. Your core business logic should be clean, focused, assumption-free. Because you've already verified everything at the doors.
Stops, turns to audience
And when something's wrong? That's where the "Fail Visibly" part comes in. Don't swallow the error. Don't try to work around it. Don't return null and hope someone notices. Pauses Throw an exception. A clear, specific, well-documented exception.
Moves center stage
Remember those specific exceptions we talked about? This is where they shine. PayPlanValidationException doesn't just tell you something's wrong - it tells you exactly what's wrong, where it's wrong, and often, how to fix it.
Gestures expansively
And here's the beautiful part - when you fail fast and fail visibly, debugging becomes... almost trivial. No more hunting through logs trying to figure out where bad data came from. No more mysterious state corruptions. No more "well, it worked yesterday..."
Takes a few steps forward
Because every failure point becomes a guardian. A clear, visible, explicit guardian that says "This shall not pass!" And more importantly, "Here's why it shall not pass!"
Thoughtful pause
But there's something else that happens when you embrace this principle. Something unexpected. Your system becomes... self-documenting.
Raises eyebrow
Think about it. Every validation, every check, every potential failure point - they're all explicit. They're all visible. They're all... documented in your exceptions.
Moves to edge of stage
Want to know what inputs your API accepts? Look at the validation exceptions. Want to know what downstream service states you handle? Check the service exceptions. Want to understand your business rules? They're right there, in your business exceptions.
Returns to center
This is why I say Programming TO Exceptions isn't just about handling errors. It's about making your system's constraints explicit. It's about turning failure modes into documentation. It's about making the invisible... visible.
Pauses, becomes more serious
And this brings us to perhaps the most important question: How do we know we're doing this right? How do we measure success?
Part 7 - Measuring Success
Moves thoughtfully to center stage
How do we know when Programming TO Exceptions is working? Pauses When your phone stops ringing at 3 AM.

Part 8 - Conclusion
Steps to center stage, speaking with quiet conviction
Let me leave you with the key tenets of Programming TO Exceptions. Not rules, not guidelines... foundations.
Pauses, lets the room settle
First: Exceptions Do Not Return. They propagate. When you throw an exception, you're not suggesting something might be wrong. You're declaring with certainty: this operation cannot continue.
Takes measured step

Steps forward

Pauses, looks around room

Moves center stage
And finally: Trust the Mechanism. Trust that exceptions will carry their context. Trust that stack unwinding will clean up resources. Trust that being explicit about failure makes your system more reliable, not less.
Takes final step forward
Programming TO Exceptions isn't just about handling errors better. It's about building systems that speak clearly. Systems that fail gracefully. Systems that guide users and developers alike through the unexpected.
Quiet, confident tone
Because at the end of the day, exceptions aren't problems to be caught. They're opportunities to be explicit about what your system can and cannot do. They're your chance to turn confusion into clarity. Chaos into confidence.
